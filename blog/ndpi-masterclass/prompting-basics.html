Prompting Basics

Nov 17, 2026 · Cody Gillespie

In the age of artificial intelligence, every working member of society should be asking themselves two questions on a routine basis:

  1. What did I spend time on last week that didn't need my expertise?
  2. What did I do last week that only I could do?

Teachers are no exception.

The job of a teacher is much like that of an architect. An architect doesn't lay every brick. He decides what the building needs to do, then makes sure it gets built right. A building's value lies in how well it serves the people who use it. The teacher, like the architect, works to make his students capable, complete human beings, and he should spend his time on the parts of that work only he can do.

So when it comes to AI, the teacher must decide what he should and should not automate. That decision is the first half of today's masterclass. The second half is learning how to ask AI for help in a way that gets you something you can actually use: the basics of writing a good prompt.

Step one: decide what to hand off

Most teaching tasks fall into one of three buckets:

  • Hand off — AI can do it
  • Assist — AI can draft it and you finish it
  • Keep — Only you can do it.

Here are some examples:

TaskBucketWhy
Writing 20 practice sentences for a grammar pointHand offRepetitive, easy to check
Making a vocabulary quiz from a readingHand offTime-consuming, easily automated
Rewriting a text at an easier levelAssistYou know what level your students can handle
Drafting a lesson planAssistAI gives a basic structure to adapt to your class
Writing feedback on an essayAssistAI can flag errors and you can decide which ones are most relevant
Noticing which student is struggling, and whyKeepDepends on knowing the student
Leading discussion in classKeepThis is the job
Deciding final gradesKeepRequires your judgment and your accountability

Once you've decided what to hand off, the quality of what comes back depends almost entirely on how you ask for it. Knowing what to ask is half the battle. Knowing how to ask is what many people miss.

Step two: ask well

A good prompt has four parts. Leave one out and the AI fills the gap with a guess.

  1. Role: who the AI should act as. "You are an experienced English teacher."
  2. Task: exactly what you want made. "Write a 200-word reading passage."
  3. Context: who it's for and why. Grade, level, topic, what students already know.
  4. Format: what the output should look like. A table, a numbered list, an answer key at the end.

Here's the difference in practice.

Weak prompt

Make a quiz about the past simple.

You'll get a quiz that is either too short or too long, and is not matched to the level of your students. You will then need to spend additional time fixing the quiz.

Strong prompt

You are an experienced English teacher. Write a 10-question
multiple-choice quiz on the past simple for B1 university students.
Use irregular verbs they're likely to know (go, see, buy, take).
Each question should be one sentence with four answer choices.
Put the answer key at the end.

Now the AI has nothing to guess about. The result will be much closer to being ready to use.

Another trick: having examples of what you want is one of the best ways to get better output. Paste your example in and prompt along the lines of "Make five more questions like this one". This beats any description.

Prompts you can use tomorrow

A reading text at the right level

You are an English teacher. Write a 200-word text about a family
visiting a bazaar for A2 students. Use only present simple and
present continuous. Bold 8 new vocabulary words, then list them
with simple definitions.

Speaking prompts for pair work

You are an English teacher planning a speaking lesson. Give me 12
discussion questions about technology in daily life for B1 university
students. Make them open-ended, avoid yes/no questions, and order
them from easiest to hardest.

One text, two levels

Here is a text my class is reading: [paste text]. Rewrite it for
students who are struggling. Keep the same story and key vocabulary,
but use shorter sentences and simpler grammar.

Feedback on student writing

You are an English teacher giving feedback to a B1 student. Here is
their paragraph: [paste paragraph, no name]. List the three most
important errors to fix, explain each in one simple sentence, and
end with one thing they did well.

Make your own fill-in-the-blank prompts

If you use a prompt every week, don't rewrite it from scratch each time. Write it once with blanks or brackets, save it, and fill in the blanks each time.

To turn any prompt into a template:

  1. Start with a prompt that already gave you a good result.
  2. Find the parts that change each time, like the topic, the text or the number of questions. Replace them with [square brackets]. You can also point the AI to a file instead, like the vocabulary list from your textbook.
  3. Keep everything that stays the same written out in full: your students' level, your format, your rules.
  4. Put a short example inside each bracket so you remember what goes there.

You can also have the AI write the template for you:

I'm an English teacher. Every week I [describe the task, e.g., make a
vocabulary worksheet from the text we're reading]. Write me a reusable
prompt template for this task. Use [square brackets] for every part that
changes from week to week, with a short example inside each bracket.
Write out the parts that stay the same, like my students' level and the
format I want. Ask me any questions you need before you write it.

What comes back will look something like this:

You are an English teacher. Make a vocabulary worksheet for
[level, e.g., B1 university students] from this text: [paste text].
Pick [number, e.g., 10] words they probably don't know yet. For each
word, give a simple definition and one new example sentence. Then add
a matching exercise using the same words, with an answer key at the end.

Keep your templates together in one place, like a document or a notes app, with a clear name for each. Routine work will evaporate.

Going bigger: a review game for your class

Prompts don't have to stop at text. AI can build a working classroom tool, like a Jeopardy-style review game you run from a projector. Kahoot, for example, is a fantastic tool, but building a new quiz by hand every time is taxing. To this I say: don't. Save your time by having your favorite AI tool do the hard work.

It uses the same four parts as before, with more detail under each. Fill in every bracket, then paste the whole thing in.

ROLE
You are an experienced [subject, e.g., economics] teacher who builds
classroom review games.

TASK
Build a Jeopardy-style review game as a single self-contained HTML file
(HTML, CSS, and JavaScript in one file, no external libraries, images,
or fonts, so it works offline).
- Topic: [e.g., supply and demand]
- After playing, students should be able to: [e.g., predict how a price
  change affects quantity demanded]
- Categories (one column each): [list 4-5]
- 5 questions per category, worth 100 to 500 points, getting harder as
  the value rises.
- An easy question looks like: [example]
- A hard question looks like: [example]
- Do not include: [e.g., anything on elasticity, trick wording]
- Every question must have one clearly correct answer.

CONTEXT
- Students: [e.g., 2nd-year university students]
- Language for all game text, questions, and answers: [e.g., Uzbek, Latin script]
- Common mistakes to target with some questions: [e.g., confusing a shift
  of the curve with movement along it]
- Number of teams: [e.g., 3], with editable team names
- Played on one projector, with the teacher clicking. Use large text that
  is readable from the back of a classroom.
- Target game length: [e.g., 20 minutes]

FORMAT
- A board of categories and point values. Clicking a tile opens the
  question full-screen.
- A "Show answer" button reveals the answer with a one-sentence explanation.
- After the answer, buttons to award the points to a team, or close with
  no points. Wrong answers [lose / do not lose] points.
- [A 30-second countdown timer on each question / No timer]
- Used tiles are greyed out. A scoreboard is always visible. When all
  tiles are used, show the winner.
- A reset button to start a new game.
- Keep all questions and answers in one clearly labeled list at the top
  of the script so I can edit them by hand later.

Before building, list the questions and answers for me to review.

When it's done, save it as a file ending in .html and open it in any web browser. If you're using Claude, you can also open it right in the chat.

Common mistakes

AI makes mistakes, and it makes them confidently. You're still the teacher, so everything it gives you gets checked before it reaches students.

  • Leaving out the level. Without it, you'll get text that's too hard or too easy. Always name the level.
  • Accepting the first answer. If it's not right, say what's wrong: "Too long, cut it to 100 words." "Make question 4 easier." Treat it like a conversation, not a vending machine.
  • Trusting the answer key. AI sometimes marks the wrong answer as correct. Take the quiz yourself before you hand it out.
  • Trusting facts. Dates, names, statistics and quotes can be invented. Check anything students will learn as fact.
  • Sharing student information. Never paste in student names or personal details. Remove them first.

Try it

  1. Write down three tasks you did last week.
  2. Put each one in a bucket: hand off, assist, or keep.
  3. Pick one task you'd hand off. Write the shortest prompt you can for it.
  4. Now rewrite it with all four parts: role, task, context, format.
  5. Run both. Compare the results with a partner.

The point

An architect is not a mason. Nor a plumber, electrician, or any of the many trades that put up his buildings. He decides what the building needs and makes sure it gets built right. Use AI for the bricks, and spend the time you get back on the work only you can do: knowing your students and helping them grow.
